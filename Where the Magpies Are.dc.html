Did the blue magpie move?

Ninety times more people saw it in the 2020s than in the 1990s. Its range barely shifted. Here is how those two facts live together — and why the first one is not about birds at all.

The subject

A loud blue bird that lives in one wet corner of one island

The Sri Lanka blue magpie — Urocissa ornata — lives nowhere else on Earth. It needs tall, wet, undisturbed rainforest, which on this island means a shrinking band in the southwest. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable.

So when a bird like this appears to be spreading, or retreating, people pay attention. Which is exactly why it matters whether the pattern is real.

Synthetic reference imagery · not a field photograph

The question sounds simple. Has this bird's range moved in thirty-four years?

To answer it you need to know where the bird was, then, and where it is, now. Nobody ran a thirty-four-year survey. What exists instead is a pile of sightings — birders, researchers, tourists, each writing down a place and a date. Three thousand six hundred and twenty of them.

The evidence, honestly

Those sightings are not spread evenly. Not even close.

What the sightings look like

Smudge each sighting into a cloud, and a shape appears

A single dot on a map tells you almost nothing. So each sighting is blurred into a small cloud — about a kilometre wide — and all the clouds are added together. Dark means many overlapping sightings. Pale means few or none.

Press play and watch thirty-four years pass. The early frames are grainy on purpose: when a map rests on twenty-two sightings, it should not look as confident as one resting on two thousand.

What you are watching fill in is not a bird spreading out. It is a map being discovered.

“More sightings means more people looking. It does not mean more birds.”
The answer

In thirty-four years, the centre of gravity moved about as far as you could walk in a morning

Take every sighting in a five-year window and find its balance point — the place the map would tip on. Track that point across seven windows and it wanders {{ netKm }} km from start to finish, never straying more than {{ maxKm }} km from where it began.

On a map of the whole study area that movement is invisible — you have to magnify a 16-kilometre box nine times before it becomes a picture at all. And the earliest points, the ones drawn from a handful of sightings, could sit anywhere. This is wobble, not migration.

Why bother saying nothing happened?

Because the shape is seductive

Run the same data through a model that ignores effort and you get a confident story about expansion. It would be publishable. It would also be an artefact of birdwatchers buying phones.

Because decisions follow

Range maps feed protected-area boundaries and threat assessments. A false expansion makes a vulnerable species look safer than it is.

Because the fix is known

Effort-corrected models, repeat surveys along fixed routes, detection probabilities. None of it is exotic. It just has to be done before the map is drawn, not after.

The honest headline is the boring one: a stable core around Sinharaja, and a record set that grew up around it.

That is not a failure of the analysis. It is the analysis working — refusing to turn thirty-four years of uneven attention into a story about a bird that never asked to be counted.
